Shop notes

Nail gun safety: use single-shot mode and keep your hands back. Today’s featured deal is a cordless brad nailer, and it’s worth remembering how often these tools hurt people. NIOSH says nail guns are behind about 37,000 emergency-room visits a year, mostly puncture wounds to the hands and fingers. The biggest factor is the trigger. A contact or “bump” trigger fires whenever the nose is pressed while the trigger is held, so a recoil bounce can fire a second nail you didn’t intend. The full sequential (single-shot) trigger is the safest: press the nose to the work, then pull the trigger, and release both before the next nail. Many nailers, including today’s Bosch, have a mode switch, so leave it in single-shot for furniture and trim work. The OSHA/NIOSH contractor guide also says to keep the hand that holds the work at least 12″ from the nailing point, to use a hammer instead when you can’t reach the work comfortably or are working overhead or in a tight space, and to keep your finger off the trigger when you’re not nailing. Before you clear a jam or set the tool down, disconnect the power source (pull the battery on a cordless nailer). Wear safety glasses, and watch for knots and thin stock where a brad can deflect or blow out the side. Get medical care right away for any nail gun injury, even one that looks minor. Sources: CDC/NIOSH — Nail Gun Safety · OSHA/NIOSH — Nail Gun Safety: A Guide for Construction Contractors (PDF).

Build Tips

1. Dial in box-joint fit on full-width test pieces before you cut the real parts. Whether you use a router-table jig like today’s JessEm or a dado stack and a homemade sled, the spacing between the index key and the cutter decides the fit. Woodsmith points out that the gap between a loose joint and a tight one can be just a few thousandths of an inch. Cut two test pieces the same thickness and width as your project parts, so you check the whole run of fingers and not just one. Cut the first slot with the edge against the key, then flip that piece, hook its slot over the key, butt the mating piece against it, and work across both as a pair. If there are gaps, move the key away from the cutter. If the pins won’t go together, move it toward the cutter. Change it in tiny steps (WOOD Magazine just taps the jig with a hammer) and recut. WOOD also raises the cutter about .016″ more than the finger size calls for, so the fingers stand slightly proud and sand flush after glue-up, and it starts with stock about 1/8″ wider than needed so it can rip off a partial finger at the end. Label the mating parts and mark the edge that starts against the key, and follow the same order you used on the test pieces.

Woodsmith — How to Make Box Joints on a Table Saw · WOOD Magazine — Box-Joint Jig

2. (Staining/finish coloring) Mix your own stain color, the controlled way. When no stock color matches the piece you’re trying to match, blend two. General Finishes says like stains can be mixed at any ratio: water-based with water-based, oil with oil. Don’t wet-mix a water-based stain with an oil-based one. Its rule of thumb is to stay within one brand, because products are formulated to work as a system. Gel stains can be blended with each other too, and GF says adding about 10% of its liquid oil-based stain makes a gel slightly thinner. Stir each can thoroughly first so the settled pigment is back in suspension, or your ratio is off before you start. Then measure the parts, with a syringe or a marked cup, and write the recipe on the sample board so you can mix the second batch to match. Test the blend on scrap of the same wood, sanded to the same grit, with the topcoat you’ll actually use, because GF notes that the wood itself changes the color and the topcoat deepens it. Mix enough for the whole project in one batch.
