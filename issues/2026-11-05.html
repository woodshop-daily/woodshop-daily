Shop notes

Heating a shop this winter: give heaters 3 feet of clear space. The U.S. Fire Administration says heating is the second leading cause of home fires, and portable heaters, only about 3% of heating fires, account for 41% of the deadly ones. Keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet from any heat source. In a woodshop that includes offcuts, sawdust piles, rags, finish cans and the dust-collector bag. Turn space heaters off when you leave the shop. Sweep or vacuum dust away from the heater before you switch it on, keep solvent-based finishes and their vapors well away from open-flame or glowing-element heaters, and have fuel-burning heaters and stoves inspected every year. Source: U.S. Fire Administration (FEMA) — Heating Fire Safety; NFPA — Portable Space Heater Safety Tips.

Build Tips

1. Rout dadoes that actually fit your plywood. “3/4″” hardwood plywood usually measures close to 1/32″ thin, and the standard allows up to 3/64″ of variation, so a 3/4″ bit leaves a sloppy dado. Rockler suggests two fixes. The quick one is an undersized plywood bit (23/32″ for 3/4″ ply, 15/32″ for 1/2″ ply), which gets close but not always exact. For a dead-on fit, use the two-pass method: clamp a straightedge or track guide, rout one pass with a smaller straight bit, then add a spacer equal to the actual panel thickness minus the bit diameter and rout the second pass. Measure the actual sheet you’re using with calipers, make the test cut in scrap, and check the fit with an offcut of the shelf before you rout the real sides.

Rockler — Getting Dadoes and Plywood Thickness to Match · WOOD Magazine — Use Your Router to Cut Accurate Dadoes for Plywood

2. (Staining/finish coloring) Hide walnut sapwood with dye, not stain. Pale sapwood streaks on walnut boards stand out more once you finish them. WOOD Magazine and Fine Woodworking both recommend dye rather than a pigment stain to color just the sapwood, because dye colors the wood evenly without muddying the grain. Wet a heartwood area with a damp cloth or alcohol to preview its finished color, then mix and dilute dye on scrap until the sapwood matches it. Brush the dye along the sapwood following the grain and feather the edge into the heartwood with a damp rag. If you used water-based dye, lightly knock down the raised grain with 220 grit once it’s dry. For a more uniform board, dye the sapwood first, then wipe a light, warm brown stain or dye over the whole piece before the clear coat.
