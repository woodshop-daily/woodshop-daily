Shop notes

Lathe safety: check the speed, stand out of the line of fire, and wear a full face shield. With a midi lathe as today’s featured deal, it’s worth going over what the American Association of Woodturners calls the rules that matter most. Wear a full face shield, not just safety glasses, whenever the lathe is on. Tie back long hair, and skip gloves, loose sleeves and jewelry. Before you switch on, tighten the tool-rest and tailstock locks, take the chuck key out, spin the work by hand to be sure it clears the rest and the bed, and check the speed. AAW’s spindle guide is about 3,000 rpm for stock 1″ or less, 2,000 rpm at 2″, 1,500 rpm at 3″, and 1,000 rpm or less at 4″ and up. Its rule of thumb: when in doubt, the speed is probably too high, so start slow and speed up only once the piece is balanced. Step out of the “red zone,” the area in line with the spinning work where a blank goes if it comes loose, and keep a hand on the switch as you turn it on. Rest the tool on the tool rest before it touches the wood, turn the lathe off before you move the rest, and take the rest off before sanding or finishing. Never wrap a rag around your fingers to apply finish to spinning work, and never walk away until the lathe has fully stopped. The UK HSE’s lathe sheet adds that the rest should be set close to the work, and readjusted as the diameter shrinks. Sources: American Association of Woodturners — Lathe Safety Basics · AAW — Safety · HSE — Wood turning lathes: safe working practices (WIS28, PDF).

Build Tips

1. Set scroll-saw blade tension by feel (and sound), then match the blade to the stock. Most broken and wandering scroll-saw blades come down to tension. Scroll Saw Woodworking & Crafts says a properly tensioned blade should flex no more than about 1/16″ (2 mm) when you push it side to side or front to back. Many scrollers also pluck it like a guitar string and listen for a clear, high “ping” (the magazine describes it as a high C). Too loose, and the blade wanders and cuts out of square. Too tight, or pushed too hard, and it snaps. Snug the blade clamps with finger-and-thumb pressure only, because over-tightening the clamp screws can spread the jaws. If a new blade slips, rub both ends on sandpaper to take off the factory oil. For blade choice, the magazine’s rule of thumb is to keep at least four teeth in the wood: use fewer teeth per inch for thick stock and more for thin, so sawdust can clear and the blade doesn’t heat up and burn the cut. Most teeth should point down. Release the tension when you’re done for the day.

Scroll Saw Woodworking & Crafts — Tips for Easy Saw Maintenance · Scroll Saw Woodworking & Crafts — Better Scrolling with BATS · Choosing the Right Blade

2. (Staining/finish coloring) Warm up a finish with amber shellac, and know what can go over it. A coat or two of amber shellac gives pale woods like maple, birch and pine an aged, honey tone, and it warms a stained piece without another stain step. The catch is wax. Zinsser’s Bulls Eye Amber and Clear shellacs are waxy 3-lb cuts, and Zinsser’s own data sheet says Bulls Eye shellac is not recommended as a sealer under polyurethane; use its dewaxed SealCoat for that. Fine Woodworking adds that the waxy amber is fine under nitrocellulose lacquer or oil-based varnishes that aren’t polyurethane (an alkyd varnish or Waterlox, for example). Under oil-based poly or any water-based finish, stick with dewaxed shellac. One common fix is to apply the amber for color and then a coat of SealCoat as a barrier before the poly. For better control, thin the amber about 1:1 with denatured alcohol and build color in thin coats; Fine Woodworking notes that equal parts turns the 3-lb cut into roughly a 1-1/2-lb cut. Test the whole schedule (stain, amber, barrier, topcoat) on a scrap of the same wood first, because each coat deepens the color.
