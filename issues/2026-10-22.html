Shop notes

Stripping old paint? Assume it could contain lead: With a heat gun and a surface-conditioning drum on today’s list, here’s a reminder from the U.S. EPA. Paint on salvaged doors, trim and furniture from before 1978 may contain lead, and scraping, sanding or overheating it releases lead dust and fumes. If you use a heat gun, keep it at a low-temperature setting under 1,100°F and never let the paint char. Mist the surface before scraping, close off the work area with plastic sheeting, and clean up with a HEPA vacuum and wet wipes rather than a shop broom. Don’t power-sand or grind painted surfaces unless the tool is shrouded and connected to a HEPA vacuum. A lead test kit is cheap insurance before you start. Source: U.S. EPA — Lead-Safe Renovations for DIYers.

Build Tips

1. Rout in the right direction. A handheld router’s bit spins clockwise when you look down from above, so feed against the rotation. Move counterclockwise around the outside edges of a board, and clockwise around the inside of a frame or cutout, so the bearing pulls into the edge instead of skipping away. Rout the end grain first so the long-grain passes clean up any blowout at the corners. A climb cut (feeding with the rotation) can fix tearout on tricky grain, but only take very light passes with the work clamped down and both hands on the router. Never climb-cut on the router table.

WOOD Magazine — Router 101

2. (Staining/finish coloring) Ebonize with steel wool and vinegar. For a deep black that still shows the grain, color the wood chemically instead of staining it. Rinse a pack of steel wool to remove the oil, then submerge it in white vinegar for a day or two and strain the liquid through clean rags. Leave the container loosely covered, because the reaction gives off gas. The iron acetate reacts with tannin in the wood. Oak and walnut go black, and low-tannin woods turn gray, blue or purple, so test on scrap first. Sand to 220, raise the grain with water, then brush the solution on with the grain while the wood is still damp. Once it’s dry, knock back the raised grain with 400 grit. A penetrating oil finish turns the navy tone to a rich black.

Fine Woodworking (Michael Robbins) — Ebonizing Wood with Steel Wool and Vinegar
